=== Episode: "The Weight of Existence"

It was a Friday evening. Minjun sat alone in an empty office, staring blankly at his monitor. On the screen was a draft notification titled "End of Service Announcement." The service he had poured himself into, day and night for three years, was finally shutting down.

"I guess I really... can't do it."

The familiar voice echoed through his head. As he had practiced in the workshop, he recognized it — his inner gremlin. But today, it was particularly hard to quiet.

_Observe without judgment._ Recalling the technique from the previous session, he breathed in slowly.

"Right now I... feel a tightness in my chest. A lump in my throat. Grief over the failure... and..."

He looked more deeply into his own emotions. This was not simple regret over a failed project. Something more fundamental felt as though it were unraveling.

"Ah. I see — I was trying to prove that I had worth."

It suddenly became clear. He had believed he could only be a person of value if he built a successful product. The title of "skilled developer," the recognition of his colleagues, the growing user numbers — all of it had served as evidence of his worth. And now, that evidence was disappearing.

Then his phone rang. It was his five-year-old daughter, Seoyeon.

"Daddy, when are you coming home? Seoyeon misses you."

"I'll be there soon."

"Daddy, I drew a picture at preschool today. It's your face! I'm going to show you!"

After hanging up, Minjun suddenly realized something.

_Seoyeon doesn't care whether I succeeded with this project or not. To her, I'm just Dad._

He closed his eyes and let himself imagine. What if I were gone? Who would put Seoyeon to bed? Who would my wife talk through her day with? Who would give the junior developers on my team their code reviews?

But then he shook his head.

_Isn't that still worth based on roles? Worth only as a father, a husband, a mentor..._

He pulled the workshop materials out of his desk drawer. His eyes landed on the section titled "DBT Validation." He read it slowly, aloud.

"Acknowledging your own experience exactly as it is."

And then, carefully, he continued:

"Right now I... am sad because the project failed. And that is valid. It makes sense to grieve when three years of work comes to an end."

"And I feel worthless. I can understand this feeling too. I've been taught my whole life to prove my value through achievement."

He paused. Then slowly, but clearly, he said:

"But just because this feeling is understandable... does not make it true."

In that moment, Minjun felt something distinctly. Acknowledging his emotions and believing that those emotions were speaking the truth were two entirely different things.

He recalled his daughter's voice again. "I miss you, Daddy." There were no conditions in those words. Not "I miss you when you're successful, Daddy" — just "Daddy."

He read another section of the materials. It was about Viktor Frankl — a Holocaust survivor and psychiatrist:

[.quote-box]
____
Everything can be taken from a person but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances.

_— Viktor Frankl_
____

_What I can choose is how I look at this failure._

Minjun breathed in deeply.

"I failed at the project. And that is painful. But I am still me. Seoyeon's father. My wife's husband. A mentor to the junior developers."

He paused, then felt something rising from a deeper place.

"But more fundamentally than those roles... I simply exist. And that existence itself..."

The words "has worth" still felt strange to say. But at least this much he could know:

"At least my existence means something to someone. And that has nothing to do with what I've achieved."

He shut down his computer and packed his bag. He needed to go home to see the picture Seoyeon had drawn. One part of his chest still felt heavy — but alongside it, he noticed a strange lightness.

Walking down the hallway, he said quietly to himself:

"Today I am a developer who failed at his project. And at the same time, I am someone's father. That, in itself, is enough."

Just as he stepped out the office door, another message appeared on his phone.

"Daddy, I finished your drawing! Come home fast! I love you!"

Minjun smiled. It was a small smile, but it was real.

=== The Trap of Conditional Worth

Does Minjun's story feel familiar? Most of us live this way.

We feel valuable only when we *achieve* something, when we receive *approval* from someone, when we meet a certain *standard*. In psychology, this is called *Conditional Self-Worth*.

[.concept-box]
.Three Types of Conditional Self-Worth
====
1. *Performance-based*
   - "I only have worth when I achieve."
   - Examples: good grades, a promotion, a successful project

2. *Other-based*
   - "I only have worth when others recognize me."
   - Examples: praise, likes, external evaluations

3. *Attribute-based*
   - "I only have worth when I possess certain qualities."
   - Examples: appearance, educational pedigree, wealth
====

Psychologist Carl Rogers explained that this conditional valuation begins to form in childhood. When parents or teachers show love and recognition only in response to certain behaviors, a child naturally comes to believe:

[.quote-box]
____
"I am only lovable when I ___."
____

These are what Rogers called the "Conditions of Worth." And they persist into adulthood:

- "I have worth only if I perform well."
- "I only have meaning when I'm useful to others."
- "I can only be recognized if I'm perfect."
- "I only deserve love if I succeed."

==== The Sisyphean Syndrome

The problem is that this conditional valuation traps us in an endless cycle.

Even if you achieve something today, tomorrow you must prove your worth all over again. Like Sisyphus in the Greek myth, eternally pushing his boulder up the mountain only to watch it roll back down.

. *Achievement* — brief satisfaction
. ↓ *Anxiety returns* — worth must be proven again
. ↓ *Pursuit of the next achievement* — endless repetition
. ↑ The Sisyphean cycle (back to the beginning)

[TIP]
====
Conditional self-worth is like unquenchable thirst. No matter how much achievement you drink, no matter how much approval — the thirst returns.
====

'''

=== Unconditional Self-Worth: You, as You Are

So what is the alternative? It is *Unconditional Self-Worth*.

Unconditional self-worth is disarmingly simple:

[.quote-box]
____
*You have worth because you exist.*

Even if you have achieved nothing, even if no one has recognized you, even if you are far from perfect — you have worth.
____

One psychologist put it this way:

"Your worth is not leveraged by anything."

This is the concept of *Grace* — something you are deserving of, but need not earn. Your worth is not something to be proven. It is already given.

==== A Note on Misunderstanding

Unconditional self-worth does not mean achievement and effort are meaningless.

Unconditional self-worth is not "stop achieving." It is the recognition that *regardless of whether you achieve, you are already worthy.*

Can you feel the difference?

[cols="1,2", frame=all, grid=all]
|===
| *Conditional* | "I have worth only when I succeed." → Failure means worthlessness
| *Unconditional* | "I am already worthy, and success is my choice." → Worth remains intact even in failure
|===

==== The Practical Difference Between the Two

*A person living from conditional self-worth:*

- Fails an exam → "I'm incompetent" → self-criticism
- Project fails → "I'm a failure" → depression
- Partner breaks up with them → "I don't deserve to be loved" → despair

*A person living from unconditional self-worth:*

- Fails an exam → "I didn't pass this test, but I'm still myself" → room to try again
- Project fails → "This project failed, but I learned from it" → an opportunity for growth
- Partner breaks up with them → "This relationship is over, but I still deserve love" → space for healing

'''

=== DBT Self-Validation: Acknowledging Your Emotions Without Treating Them as Fact

So how do you actually experience unconditional self-worth?

This is where *DBT's concept of Validation* becomes useful.

In DBT, validation means *acknowledging one's own experience, thoughts, and emotions without judgment*.

[.concept-box]
.Validation vs. Agreement
====
A crucial point: validation is not the same as *agreement*:

- *Validation*: Acknowledging that your emotions are *valid* (understandable, legitimate)
- *Agreement*: Agreeing that your emotions are *accurate* or that acting on them would be *effective*
====

Let us return to Minjun's situation:

1. "I'm sad because I failed."
   - This emotion is *valid*. It makes sense to grieve when a three-year project ends.

2. "I feel worthless."
   - This feeling is also *understandable*. It's a natural response for someone who has been taught to prove their worth through achievement.

3. But "I am worthless" is *not a fact*.

This is the heart of self-validation: *acknowledging your emotions while not confusing them with truth.*

==== The Four Stages of Self-Validation

Drawing on DBT research, self-validation can be summarized in four stages:

. *Stage 1: Acknowledge*
+
"I feel this way right now."
+
. *Stage 2: Allow*
+
"It is okay to feel this."
+
. *Stage 3: Understand*
+
"I understand why this feeling arose."
+
. *Stage 4: Distinguish*
+
"This feeling is not speaking the truth."

*Applying it in practice:*

1. *Acknowledge*: "I feel worthless right now."
2. *Allow*: "It is natural to feel this way."
3. *Understand*: "Because I have been taught my whole life to prove my worth through achievement."
4. *Distinguish*: "Even though I feel worthless, I am still a person of worth."

These four stages map exactly onto what Minjun went through. He acknowledged his feelings (Stage 1), allowed them to be there (Stage 2), understood why they arose (Stage 3), and finally distinguished emotion from fact (Stage 4).

=== Viktor Frankl: Finding Meaning Even in Suffering

Viktor Frankl — Holocaust survivor and psychiatrist — made an important discovery in the most extreme of circumstances.

What did the people who survived the Nazi concentration camps have in common? They were people who had found *meaning* in life.

Based on this, Frankl developed *Logotherapy* and wrote:

[.quote-box]
____
Everything can be taken from a person but one thing: the last of the human freedoms — to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances.

_— Viktor Frankl_
____

==== Three Ways to Find Meaning

According to Frankl, we can discover meaning in life through three paths:

[.concept-box]
.Three Ways to Find Meaning
====
1. *Through creative work*
   - By making something or achieving something
   - Examples: art, invention, completing a project

2. *Through experience and relationship (Experiential values)*
   - By experiencing love, beauty, nature
   - Examples: relationships with people we love, experiencing moments of beauty

3. *Through our attitude toward suffering (Attitudinal values)*
   - Through how we choose to face unavoidable suffering
   - Examples: our attitude toward illness, loss, or failure
====

A key point in Frankl's thinking: *meaning is not something we create but something we discover.*

We do not ask life, "What meaning do you hold for me?" Rather, *life asks us, "What will you do?"*

[TIP]
====
Let us return to Minjun. In the midst of the pain of a failed project, he discovered one thing: that he is a meaningful presence to his daughter Seoyeon, to his wife, to his junior colleagues. And that meaning exists regardless of whether his project succeeded.
====

=== Practice: Experiencing Unconditional Self-Worth

Understanding a concept is different from experiencing it. Try these approaches this week.

==== Exercise 1: Reframing Your Self-Talk

[.practice-box]
.Shifting from Conditional to Unconditional
====
*Find your conditional worth statements:*

- "I only have worth when I ___."
- "I can only be recognized if I ___."
- "I'm useless unless I ___."

*Transform them into unconditional statements:*

- "I like ___, but even without it, I still exist."
- "___ is my choice, not what determines my worth."

*Examples:*

- Conditional: "I only have worth when I do good work."
- Unconditional: "I am already worthy, and doing good work is my choice."
====

==== Exercise 2: Recognizing the Ripple Effect of Your Existence

Ask yourself:

"If I had never existed, how would someone's life be different?"

[TIP]
====
*Important*: Do not approach this from a place of fear or guilt. Instead, approach it from a stance of *gratitude*.

"I am having a positive impact on someone's life."
====

*Examples:*

- If I hadn't existed, who would my friend have talked to in a difficult moment?
- If I hadn't existed, who would the junior colleagues have asked for advice?
- If I hadn't existed, who would my family have laughed with?

==== Exercise 3: Practicing the Four Stages of Self-Validation

The next time a difficult emotion arises, walk through these four stages:

[.practice-box]
.Self-Validation Practice
====
1. *Acknowledge*: "I feel [emotion] right now."
2. *Allow*: "It is okay to feel this."
3. *Understand*: "Because [reason]."
4. *Distinguish*: "But this feeling is not speaking the truth about my worth."

*Example:*

1. "I feel anxious right now."
2. "It is natural to feel anxious."
3. "Because I have an important presentation tomorrow."
4. "But my anxiety does not mean I am incompetent."
====

==== Exercise 4: Applying Frankl's Questions

When you find yourself in a painful situation, ask yourself these questions:

- "What meaning can I discover in this situation?"
- "What kind of person is this experience making me?"
- "What attitude can I choose to take?"

*Example:*

- Situation: Passed over for promotion
- Question: "What kind of person is this experience making me?"
- Possible answer: "Someone more humble, with a deeper understanding of what my colleagues go through."
