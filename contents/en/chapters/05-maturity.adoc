== Session 5: Maturity — A Lifelong Question

[.chapter-quote,quote]
____
Growth is expansion; maturity is distillation. Ripening over a lifetime — that is our enduring question.
____

[.concept-box]
.Core Concepts
====
- *Growth vs. Maturity*: Expansion and distillation; the wheel and the kiln
- *Self-Transcendence*: Moving beyond oneself
- *The Lifelong Question*: The question we carry throughout our lives
- *Ripening*: A direction, not a destination; a process, not a completion
====

'''

=== The Potter's Studio

When the potter Kim had not been working with clay for very long, he spent twelve hours a day at the wheel, shaping vessels. How large, how tall, how intricate — that was everything. At every exhibition he unveiled new forms, and people praised his "growth."

One day, something shifted. He could not pinpoint the exact moment. Perhaps it was when a perfectly formed piece shattered in the kiln. Or perhaps it was the strange stillness he felt while drinking tea from his master's rough, unassuming bowl. Whatever it was, after that moment the hours he spent in front of the kiln began to outnumber those at the wheel.

"Why do you waste so much time?" a young apprentice asked.

"I'm not wasting it. I'm giving it."

==== Wheel Time, Kiln Time

These days, his studio draws two kinds of visitors.

The first kind are young potters. They sit at the wheel and pull the clay upward with vigor — it grows taller, wider, taking on more and more varied forms.

"Master, this piece is the largest I've ever made!"

"This form — no one has tried it before!"

Kim nods and smiles.

"Good. Well done. But shall we put it in the kiln now?"

The second kind are his older fellow potters. They no longer speak of size or form. Instead, they talk about kiln temperature, the composition of glazes, and above all — *waiting*.

"I fired this piece three times. The first two failed. But the third time..."

"I lowered the temperature by just ten degrees, and the color changed completely. After twenty years, there is still more to learn."

Their work is not showy. Sometimes it even looks rough. But hold it in your hands and there is a particular weight to it; put it to your lips and it feels warm. Pour tea into it, and the tea tastes deeper.

[TIP]
====
At the wheel, a piece *grows*: the clay rises higher, widens, takes shape. This is a visible change.

But in the kiln, a piece *matures*: no visible change from the outside; it may even shrink a little. Yet enduring fire and time, it becomes strong, takes on color, and becomes something *that can be used*.
====

Kim sometimes wonders:

_When did I stop being someone who enlarges vessels and become someone who ripens them?_

It might happen tomorrow — the moment a perfectly formed piece shatters in the kiln. Or it might be ten years from now. Or perhaps the moment never comes at all.

*Age is not the issue. Awakening is the issue.*

A young apprentice asks:

"Master, are you a ceramic artist, or a potter?"

Kim replies:

"A ceramic artist makes things. A potter... knows how to wait. I am in the process of becoming a potter. I'm not fully there yet. I'll probably still be 'becoming' on the day I die."

'''

=== The Age of Growth, the Age of Maturity

We are fluent in the language of growth. "Faster, higher, stronger" — the Olympic motto, and the modern world's incantation. At school, grades must rise; at work, rank must climb; in bank accounts, numbers must multiply.

But at some point, this incantation begins to ring hollow. A feeling that "more" is no longer the answer. An emptiness despite accumulation.

This is the moment maturity begins to whisper.

==== Insights from Twentieth-Century Psychologists

Great psychologists who worked in different eras and on different continents were, remarkably, all pointing to the same place.

*Maslow* called it "Self-Transcendence." In the latter years of his life, he added one more stage atop his famous hierarchy of needs — placing self-transcendence above self-actualization.

[.concept-box]
.Self-Actualization vs. Self-Transcendence
====
*Self-actualization:* "What can I become?"

*Self-transcendence:* "What can I connect with, beyond myself?"

The goal is no longer the completion of "me." It is moving beyond "me" — connecting with others, with nature, with the cosmos, with a larger meaning.
====

*Jung* called it "Individuation." He drew a clear distinction: life has two phases:

1. The period of establishing the ego
2. The period of moving beyond that ego toward wholeness

[.quote-box]
____
There is no linear evolution. There is only the circumambulation of the Self.

_— Carl Jung_
____

This fundamentally changes how we think about "progress." It is not a linear march forward but a spiral circling toward the center. What appears to be a step backward may in fact be a move into greater depth.

*Erik Erikson* defined the final task of life as "Ego Integrity vs. Despair." Integrity is a deep acceptance that says "My life had to be exactly as it was" — receiving success and failure, joy and suffering, all as parts of one's life.

His most important insight: *even a person who achieves integration also experiences despair.* Integration is not a completion — it is maintaining balance between integration and despair.

*Confucius* reflected on his own life and wrote:

- "At fifty, I understood the will of Heaven." _(Knowing Heaven's purpose)_
- "At seventy, I could follow my heart's desire without transgressing what was right." _(Freedom through discipline)_

[TIP]
====
What matters is not the ages he mentions, but the process between them. Between knowing and freedom, there is a time of *ripening* that cannot be rushed.

Maturity is not the moment of awakening — it is the process of living out that awakening.
====

*Paul Tillich* distilled all of this into one phrase: "The Courage to Be." The courage to affirm existence while facing the threat of non-being — death, meaninglessness, isolation.

Maturity is not the disappearance of anxiety. It is learning to exist alongside anxiety.

[.concept-box]
====
*What all of them are saying is ultimately one thing*

Maturity is not an end — it is a direction.

Not arriving, but moving toward.

Not completion, but the endless journey toward completion.
====

'''

=== The Paradox of Maturity: Diminishing and Filling at Once

The greatest paradox of maturity is this: *maturity is both a process of letting go and a process of filling.*

Like the potter's vessel in the kiln, the piece shrinks a little. Moisture evaporates; impurities burn away. On the surface, it is a loss. But in that very process, the vessel becomes hard, takes on color, and becomes something *that can be used.*

*Diminishing into completion.*

==== The Paradox in Everyday Life

When we are young, we want many things. We want to keep all possibilities open. "I can become anything."

But at some point, we understand that we must choose. Becoming a doctor means not becoming a painter; living in New York means not living in the countryside. *To choose is to relinquish.*

And yet, paradoxically, this relinquishment sets us free. When we were grasping at all possibilities, we could do none of them well. But when we choose one and set the rest down, we can finally *live deeply* in that one thing.

This is maturity.

- The time of youth: keeping all possibilities open
- The turning point: choosing and relinquishing
- The time of maturity: living one thing deeply

Jung called this "shadow integration" — reclaiming the aspects of ourselves we have denied and suppressed. Shedding the flawless persona and fully receiving the imperfect self. This is neither addition nor subtraction. *It is distillation.*

Maslow's self-transcendence holds the same paradox. "Transcending the self-centered ego" does not mean discarding the self. On the contrary — only after establishing the self firmly can one truly move beyond it.

With empty hands, there is nothing to set down. Only when full can something overflow.

'''

=== A Lifelong Question

In Korean traditional Zen Buddhism, a *hwadu* (話頭) is a question you carry throughout your life. "What is this?" "What was your original face before your parents were born?"

Not an answer but a question. Not an arrival but a direction. Not a completion but a process.

*Maturity is exactly this kind of lifelong question.*

Even the "integration" Erikson described is not a finality, he insisted. Even someone who achieves integration sometimes experiences despair. You may wake in peace, only to feel regret wash over you by evening. What matters is maintaining the balance between the two.

Not never falling down — but getting back up after every fall.

Tillich's "Courage to Be" is also not something acquired once. It is something needed anew every morning. Yesterday's courage does not guarantee today's.

Confucius's ideal person, the *junzi* (君子), is also not something you *become* — it is something you are perpetually *becoming*.

[.quote-box]
____
So when do we mature?

The answer is simple: *until the moment we die.*

Until our last breath, we are still ripening.

There is no diploma in maturity.
____

This may sound discouraging. An endless task. Perpetual incompleteness.

But paradoxically, this is the greatest comfort.

We are freed from the pressure of becoming a "completed person." It is okay to make mistakes. It is okay to fall. It is okay to seem like less today than yesterday.

Because we are still in process.

An eighty-year-old is still moving toward maturity; a twenty-year-old already experiences moments of it. It is not a question of age — it is a question of direction. Not of arrival — but of the journey.

'''

=== The Gifts Maturity Brings

So what does this endless journey give us? If we never arrive, what do we receive?

==== 1. The Peace of Existence

We are freed from the pressure of becoming something, achieving something, proving something. A deep acceptance that *existing as oneself is enough.*

We no longer seek external recognition. We recognize ourselves.

The potter Kim is no longer shaken by the reviews at exhibitions. A good review brings joy, but a bad one does not shatter him. He already knows, standing before the kiln, whether his work is good.

==== 2. The Depth of Relationship

We no longer see others as means to our own achievement. Maslow's "B-love" — love for the person as they are.

It is not what the other person does for me that matters — their very existence is a gift.

What is full cannot be hidden. From a mature person, compassion, wisdom, and warmth flow naturally. Without effort. Without any attempt to display them.

==== 3. Acceptance of Death

The wisdom Erikson spoke of is not the absence of fear of death. When life is received as it is, death too is received as part of life.

The potter knows: no matter how lovingly crafted, every piece will one day break — dropped by someone, or simply worn away by time. But that does not diminish the vessel's value.

If anything, its finitude makes it more precious.

==== 4. Fullness in the Present Moment

When we pursue growth, we always live in the future. "Someday, when I..."

But when we move toward maturity, we live *here and now*. Because *maturity is not a future goal — it is a present attitude*.

Every moment is an opportunity for maturity:

- Not suppressing anger but fully feeling it
- Not pushing down sorrow but passing through it
- Not grasping at joy but letting it flow

All of this is maturity.

'''

=== Between the Wheel and the Kiln

So what should we do? Concretely, today?

Let us return to the potter.

The young potter must turn the wheel. Shape vessels, develop forms, master techniques. This is the time of growth. *This time cannot be skipped.* A person who has never turned the wheel has nothing to put in the kiln.

But at some point, the kiln becomes more important. Temperature, time, waiting. This is the time of maturity. Ignore this time, and no matter how beautifully formed the piece, it will remain only dried clay.

[.concept-box]
====
*The important thing is that both times are necessary.*

Without growth, there is nothing to mature.

But without maturity, growth has no meaning.
====

What is even more important is that *these two times are not divided by age.* A twenty-five-year-old can have moments of maturity; a seventy-five-year-old still has areas in which growth is needed.

More precisely, within the same person, different areas move at different paces:

- In work and career, you may be in the time of maturity already, while in relationships you still carry the tasks of growth
- In artistic expression, you may be living kiln time, while in financial management you are still turning the wheel

Maturity is not "something that comes automatically with age." Rather, *it begins at the moment of awakening.* And that awakening can come at any time.

'''

=== Practice Exercises

==== Exercise 1: Asking Yourself

So how do you know? Which time are you in right now?

[.practice-box]
.Self-Examination Questions
====
*Am I accumulating right now, or am I distilling?*

*Do I want more of things, or deeper things?*

*Do I want to display, or to share?*

*Do I want recognition, or do I already feel enough?*

If the first of each pair: you are in growth time.

If the second: maturity is calling you.

*Both are needed. Both are beautiful.*
====

==== Exercise 2: A Morning Question

Maturity is not something for the distant future. It can begin today, in this moment.

[.practice-box]
.Morning Check-In
====
Every morning, ask yourself:

"What will I grow today — or what will I ripen?"

- Growing: a new skill, a new relationship, a new experience
- Ripening: existing things, more deeply, more slowly, more fully
====

==== Exercise 3: Catching the Turning Point

[.practice-box]
.Finding the Starting Point of Maturity
====
Has there been a moment in your life when you shifted from wanting "more" to wanting "deeper"?

Write it down. That is the beginning of maturity.

Examples:

- A moment when you wanted a deep friendship over more friends
- A moment when you wanted to complete one project rather than take on many
- A moment when your own peace of mind became more important than recognition
====

==== Exercise 4: Weekly Reflection

[.practice-box]
.Finding Balance
====
Once a week, write down one thing you "expanded" and one thing you "distilled" this week.

*What I expanded:* (growth)

*What I distilled:* (maturity)

If only one is present, look for ways to restore the balance.
====

==== Exercise 5: Area-by-Area Check-In

[.practice-box]
.Checking Which Time Each Area Is In
====
In each area of life, am I in wheel time or kiln time right now?

- *Work/Career:* ☐ Wheel (expanding) / ☐ Kiln (deepening)
- *Relationships:* ☐ Wheel (expanding) / ☐ Kiln (deepening)
- *Health:* ☐ Wheel (expanding) / ☐ Kiln (deepening)
- *Spiritual life:* ☐ Wheel (expanding) / ☐ Kiln (deepening)

The same person can be in different stages in different areas.
====

==== Exercise 6: Honoring Kiln Time

[TIP]
====
Do not fear the times when nothing seems to be happening.

In the kiln, invisible change is taking place.

Whether you are twenty or sixty, this may be your kiln time.

Waiting is work. Ripening is also growth.
====

==== Exercise 7: Paying Attention to Awakening

Maturity is not brought by age — it is brought by awakening.

When the moment comes that says, "From now on, things need to be different" — do not dismiss it.

That is your own "understanding the will of Heaven." Whether you are twenty or fifty.

=== A Final Word: Living with the Question

A young potter who has come to Kim's studio asks:

"Master, when will I be able to be like you?"

Kim smiles quietly.

"Like me? I haven't managed to become 'like me' yet."

"What do you mean? You're already a master."

"A master? That's just a name other people give. Every morning when I open the studio door, I think to myself: 'What will I learn today?' And every evening when I close the door, I think: 'Ah, I learned again today.'"

"So... you are still learning?"

"Of course. Learning for a lifetime. That's what this work is."

"Then when does it become complete?"

Kim thinks for a moment before answering.

"Complete? There is no completion. Or more precisely — the moment of death is the completion. Before that, everything is process."

"So it's always unfinished? Is that a good thing?"

"Of course it is. Because that's what life is. Life is not something that gets completed — it is something that is lived. Day by day. You'll understand someday. There comes a moment when you're turning the wheel and suddenly the kiln becomes more important. It might be tomorrow, or it might be ten years from now. The important thing is recognizing the moment when it comes."

"How will I know?"

"Hmm... how to put it. Maybe when you no longer want to show people what you've made, and instead want to share it? When the desire for recognition gives way to a feeling of already being enough? I imagine it's something like that."

The young potter nods. He does not fully understand yet. But someday, he will.

[.concept-box]
====
*Core Insight*

Maturity is a direction, not a destination.

A process, not a completion.

A lifelong question.

May you carry this question with you today.

Whether you are twenty, fifty, or seventy.

When it is time to turn the wheel, turn it with vigor.

When it is time to wait at the kiln, wait with patience.

When it is time to grow, grow with passion.

When it is time to ripen, ripen quietly.

And remember:

Maturity does not come automatically with age.

It begins, right now, with awakening.

And so it goes — until the very last moment.
====
